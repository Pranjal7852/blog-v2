From the very beginning and as far as I can remember, I always wanted to be a software engineer. From pleasing my parents on getting the first computer of the house so that I could play video games, I always liked being surrounded by technology. As a matter of fact, I did not care about which college or which course I was gonna study (I ended up getting Electronics :P). I was just so passionate about tech, I knew I would make a career in it. I find it funny for people like me when you go to social events that you are asked what your hobby is, and I cannot think of programming as being it.

![End of Day 1 at Dukaan HQ](/images/getting-into-startups-and-my-first-9-5-day1-1.jpg)

So, like everyone around me, that dream and manifestation came down to my choice on choosing my type of companies to work for during the final days of my college. As I have already talked about, I had been doing quite some development from the very beginning of my college, sometimes even for volunteering or just freelancing for a small business or to learn something new. I had the choice of choosing a large company with thousands of employees and multiple offices across the globe and country, but a good mentor of mine in college recommended me to go for a small place to start from in my career as you get to learn a lot. On the day of the farewell of his class, I asked him for one piece of advice for someone like me curious to start a career in tech, and he introduced me to YC companies and the world of startups solving problems and creating a big impact.

Before continuing, I would like to clarify why it is true and golden advice to work in a startup when you are just starting your career in tech. In startups or small companies, there are fewer employees and very few paying customers, which means it is very crucial to keep making progress and show growth to investors, otherwise you are gonna fall behind. In order to keep up with the pace, there is very little room for experimentation and often your day ends by showing some tangible progress. Also, there are no fixed roles since the management cannot hire a lot of people when starting out, so you gotta wear multiple hats and achieve goals.

![coding setup final year of college](/images/college_setup.jpg)

This advice kinda stuck with me when making the decision at the end, and of course, I chose the startup (a 1-year-old startup with 7 people running everything) as my first place to work. The interview process was very different: no LeetCode rounds, no code syntax. Only pure product thinking and general problem-solving skills, and talking about what I had built till now. The company had a great vision and product and was looking for someone who can work long hours, 7 days a week on multiple stacks: deployment, observability, product UI, frontend, backend—you name it and the name of the tech was on the job description. We didn't have our own office and shared a space with Dukaan (a mid-size startup whose founder started his career by joining the previous startup of my current founder lol). Even the HR who was coordinating my interview was a third-party recruiter company who took care of them. There were no fixed working hours, no future plan, no employee benefits, nothing. The salary was decent, I would say more than the avg package at my college. You even gotta use your own laptop, and they wanted me to relocate to Bangalore, and I ended up spending my own money for the entire relocation. My other friends told me to wait, you will get a better one, do not fall for this.

So you may think, why the hell do I want to join a company like this and still call it a dream place to be? It is because in my mind, I wanted to meet these people (giga chads) that are so productive and efficient, that are managing a product with so little manpower. Working there and living in Bangalore was really so fun. Moving around the streets in Bangalore, you would see big tech offices, everyone around you working in some kind of tech company. You get to visit your friends' offices. Like, I had friends in Google, Nvidia, Salesforce, and I enjoyed visiting their offices, and what a vibe it was. For the first time, you are living outside your home and you start to earn your own. It is not easy for sure, but it is really worth it. Ok, coming back to the work and my experience.

My manager was like a marshal in the field. He had been in the company from the very beginning and knew every bit of the product and code. He was highly efficient and made sure none of us were fooling around and wasting crucial company time. He would not like to see any of us free for even 1 sec, and always pushed us not only to research about a topic but also to implement it and deploy it at the end of the day. There was one unhealthy trait of public shaming if something wasn't done by the end of the day. Apart from all the things that were going inside the company and the unhealthy work culture, there were some things which I learned from him, and that style of working still helps me excel in whatever I do today. Although some of you reading this would call it okay, it was not; it was not okay. At times I had to cancel my plans because I was working in the office till 9 pm. Like every day, pulling 13 hours was very normal for everyone. The mindset of other peeps around me was also like that; if you are awake the whole night solving a feature that the founder asked for, congratulations, you own Legend status and now are at the top of the code chain.

But now that I think of it: it was the best place to start your career. And this mindset of putting something on a plate (I like to call it primal survivor mode) and getting things done is an attitude that is very demanding. People do not care how many hours you put in, how many lines of code you wrote, or how fast your code compiles. At the end, what matters is how fast you are able to meet business problems through code. And yes, this would mean you end up creating a system that is not perfect; you do not know many parts of it, but it is sustainable. Once you have the right resources, you can always invest in fixing things; what matters is to keep your boat on the move.

![View from nvidia office bangalore](/images/view_of_bangalore.jpg)

These are some of my meeting notes from my time working there:

1. There is no room for excuses on deadlines
2. Let's say you are given a task on a new tech which you are not familiar with. You are told to do something and you research about it, try to run a small proof of concept in a sandbox, and you find out this is not possible, the task needs to change its scope, or we gotta use some other method/tech to solve the problem. Then the best time to break this news is not at the time when everyone is expecting your work; it is at the point you just discovered it. -> This also resonates with the Agile methodology of work.
3. I used to think that my work was finished, and I would break this news and my work is done, which is actually true, but the timing really matters. But you gotta own the system as you own your car or house. Have a caring attitude towards it, look for bottlenecks, and most importantly, improve it when something touches it.
4. This not only makes you a great engineer and PROBLEM SOLVER, but it also does not push deadlines with the product.
5. Never over-commit and always try to over-deliver -> This habit creates a good impression and also increases confidence in teams.
6. Also, to quote my manager, he always used to say if you are doing work of 5 bucks, it should look as if 50 bucks were spent on it. Presentation matters. You don't have to fake it, but present it concisely in a way that resonates with the product.
7. Not to make a random statement: Always back what you are saying or drafting with observations, data, and facts.
8. Always answer in QA format: This leaves less room for ambiguity and helps move faster. Also, when done on public channels, this allows other people to give feedback and support you early if there is an offset.
	1. What I know
	2. What I don't know
	3. Whom should I ask
9. Distill your information in simple words as if everyone around you is still in their first year of college and would understand what you wrote.
